The problem

Reports go in. Nothing comes back out.

Municipalities already run reporting channels. What they do not run is a way for the resident to find out what happened, or to say when a repair was not actually done. That single gap is what kills trust and stops people reporting at all.

How it works

Four steps, and the last one is the point

Most reporting apps stop at step three. Ours does not close a fault until the person who reported it says it is actually fixed.

STEP 01

Report

A resident picks a category, drops a location and adds a photo. It takes under a minute and works on an app, a USSD menu or with no signal at all.

STEP 02

Route

The report is classified and sent to one of three response functions — Infrastructure, Water, or Safety and Community Policing — with a reference number back to the resident immediately.

STEP 03

Repair

The ward dashboard maps every open fault, colour-coded by how long it has been waiting against the City's own service standards. Crews are assigned and the resident watches the status change.

STEP 04

Confirm

When a crew marks a fault resolved, the system does not close it. It goes back to the resident, who confirms or disputes. A disputed repair reopens instead of closing.

Features

Built around what actually goes wrong

Every feature here traces back to a requirement in our project charter, and most of them exist because an earlier municipal app failed without them.

Five fault types, one platform

Water and sanitation, electricity and streetlights, roads and stormwater, waste and dumping, and safety hazards. One reporting engine, three response teams.

Requirement R3

Repair confirmation

No fault closes on the system until the resident who reported it confirms the repair. Dispute it and the report reopens. This is the one thing that rebuilds trust.

Requirement R7

Offline reporting

Parts of Ward 95 have weak coverage. The app saves the report on the phone and uploads it by itself when signal returns. The resident is told twice: saved, then sent.

Requirement R2

USSD for everyone else

Dial a short code on any handset, on any network, with no data and no app. Over 20 million South Africans already use USSD for banking and SASSA.

Requirement R1

Safety reports that reach someone

Crime and hazard reports route to the Community Policing Forum, neighbourhood watch and SAPS contacts, with anonymous reporting for people who need it.

Requirements R17, R20

Ward performance in public

A monthly report shows how many faults came in, how long they took, and the verified resolution rate — the share of repairs residents actually confirmed.

Requirement R12

POPIA by design

We collect only what a report needs, ask permission at the point of reporting, and control who sees what by role. A resident sees their own reports and nobody else's.

Requirements R8, R16

Water loss, counted

Every water fault carries an estimated loss rate, so the ward can see litres per day running out of unrepaired leaks and put a rand figure on fixing them faster.

Requirement R10
Reach

Three ways in, because not everyone has data

Reporting apps fail here when they assume a smartphone and an active data bundle. The households with the worst service problems are usually the ones with the least data.

01 / APP

Android app

Full reporting with GPS location, a photo and live status tracking. Built for the people who do have a smartphone, which is most but nowhere near all of the ward.

Available in more than one language
02 / USSD

Dial a short code

Works on any handset, on any network, with no data and no app. The same menu on a twelve-year-old Nokia as on a new phone, and the habit is already there.

03 / OFFLINE

No signal, no problem

Who benefits

One platform, four groups better off

CommunityWatch is not only a tool for residents. It gives the municipality cleaner data, the ward office evidence, and safety structures a direct line in.

Residents

The people living with the fault finally get an answer.

  • Report in under a minute, no phone queue
  • A reference number they can actually find again
  • Live status instead of silence
  • The final say on whether it was fixed

The municipality

Cleaner data in, fewer wasted trips out.

  • Duplicate reports merged into one incident
  • Faults sorted by age against service standards
  • Crews stop being sent to jobs already done
  • Exportable into the existing work-order system

The ward office

Evidence instead of anecdote at every meeting.

  • A live map of every open fault in the ward
  • A monthly report that can be shown publicly
  • A verified resolution rate, not just a closed count
  • Something concrete to escalate with

Safety structures

Community policing gets information it can act on.

  • Hazard and crime reports routed directly
  • Anonymous reporting for people at risk
  • Dark streetlights on school routes flagged urgent
  • A monthly safety summary to the CPF
The pilot

Why Ward 95, Diepsloot

We chose one ward rather than a whole city on purpose. Ward 95 has both formal and informal housing, an active ward committee we can actually work with, and every one of our five fault types occurring in it. It is also exactly the kind of place where the smartphone-and-data assumption breaks down, which is why the USSD line is not an optional extra here.

Running a pilot in one ward keeps the scope honest. We are not repairing infrastructure, replacing municipal systems or rolling out nationally. We are proving that a closed reporting loop changes behaviour, in a place where it matters.
